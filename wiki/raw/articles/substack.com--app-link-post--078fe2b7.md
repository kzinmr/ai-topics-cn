# ChinAI #375: Critiquing Anthropic’s Variant of Pacing the Frontier

Source: https://substack.com/app-link/post?publication_id=2660&post_id=216491086&utm_source=substack&utm_medium=email&isFreemail=true&comments=true&token=eyJ1c2VyX2lkIjo0MDg3NDgyLCJwb3N0X2lkIjoyMTY0OTEwODYsImlhdCI6MTc4OTk4OTQ2MSwiZXhwIjoxNzkyNTgxNDYxLCJpc3MiOiJwdWItMjY2MCIsInN1YiI6InBvc3QtcmVhY3Rpb24ifQ.u3aWeFbX0oJxJYmI0LhtEZs9XkJRTEB3Nfin4_xp9GY&r=2flx6&utm_campaign=email-half-magic-comments&action=post-comment&utm_source=substack&utm_medium=email

> there’s some logical tension between this argument and Anthropic’s indignation over distillation. For the sake of argument, let’s assume Dario’s claims about distillation are true: it is a key driver behind how Chinese competitors catch up to U.S. frontier models. Well then, if U.S. companies slowed down their development of leading models, then this would necessarily also slow down the pace of Chinese frontier AI development, since they would be distilling lower-quality models at a slower rate.
This is true, and I think the way to understand it is by adding a detail Dario doesn't want to say: Chinese companies are quite competent. They *are* using distillation, lots of it, but they are *also* building out plenty of domestic expertise and therefore would continue advancing (at a slower rate) even if all the U.S. companies paused. I figure if Chinese companies are 5 months behind the U.S. now, but U.S. companies hypothetically paused, then Chinese companies might catch up in 12 months. Obviously, Dario doesn't want that and will continue the AI race to a greater or lesser extent, absent an agreement with China.
> The path between initial discovery and diffusion into widespread use is a long road. Malaria is a curable disease, but 600,000 people still die from it each year.
Whether to eradicate malaria was[1], and still is, a choice. We even have multiple ways to do it.[2] It could have been done by now, and I think we could do it pretty quickly if people got together and made that choice. We wouldn't even have to eradicate most of the mosquitos. The same sort of reasoning applies somewhat to AI: we can do it fast or slow. The difference is that in AI, various structural factors tend to put a minimum speed on progress, and that minimum isn't slow.
> In January 2025, Dario told us that transformative AI would arrive in 2026. Then, in May 2026, Anthropic assured us that AI’s decisive impact on global leadership would occur in 2028. Now, Dario predicts that “the window when AI becomes geopolitically most important” is the 2029-2031 period.
Anthropic's AIs have been transformative to our company. That's a fact.
AI certainly hasn't transformed everything. But he said it would *arrive* in 2026, not transform everything in 2026. Transformative, not transformed. I actually saw a video today titled called "Why AI hasn't made more economic growth" and I didn't click it because the answer seemed so obvious. We *are* getting growth by building datacenters, but the economy is otherwise sluggish, what with the Hormuz closure and trade wars connected to mysterious orange forces. These things take time. It'll go like this:
1. Software companies will clear out their backlogs and build more things than they historically could (happening now). But this provides limited growth, since limitless conventional software doesn't provide limitless value to the world.
2. More and more people will ask Astra and Fable to do tasks for them on their computers, and see things they've never seen before. They should be impressed; whether or not they are is a different matter. Meanwhile, AI companies will figure out how to make general robot intelligence (conventional LLMs can't hack it there) while all kinds of companies will find new ways to use AI to design components, design products, and design factories, which should provide some economic growth, but still not enough for the general public to feel yet.
3. Factories (mostly in China) will churn out dextrous and probably bipedal robots for domestic, business, and eventually military tasks. At first they will tend to be split intelligences, with a fast and dumb onboard model paired with a cloud supercomputer. Meanwhile, absent a "pause", models will keep improving extremely quickly in both China and the U.S., though opposition to data center and power plant construction may impact (only) the latter.
4. The robots will start doing previously human jobs, eventually including construction of robot factories. This, then, could lead to the Pikkety/Philip Trammell scenario.[3] Meanwhile, AIs will be helping cure diseases, though "5–10 years" sounds pretty optimistic to me because biology seems somewhat intractable computationally, and in-vivo human experiments seem inevitably slow and costly. Still, anti-cancer vaccines are already finding success and AI might well unlock some shortcuts to curing major diseases, both on the computational side (just as they have helped a lot by predicting protein folds) and on the physical side (performing some lab tasks).
The big question: how fast? I'm confident it'll be faster than most people will want, including a lot of staff at AI companies. Oh, and Recursive Self Improvement is dangerous. We really shouldn't.[4]
