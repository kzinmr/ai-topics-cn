Context:
Many of you may be familiar with China’s “996” work culture (9AM to 9PM, six days a week). In recent years, we’ve seen some opposition to this brutal schedule. This Perpetual Light Studio [极昼工作室] longform article explores a trend of young people rejecting jobs with six-day work weeks. I like these Perpetual Light Studio pieces because they are always rooted in many interviews with a diverse range of sources. In a previous ChinAI issue, I translated their article about the intrusions of AI surveillance in Chinese universities (
ChinAI #357
)
Key Passages: As a human resources employee at a mid-sized internet company, Annie
keeps getting rejected by job candidates who want a two-day weekend. Keeping track of he own statistics, she found that “over 80% of candidates who received job offers ultimately declined the positions because the company only offered one day off per week.”
She pushed her manager to change the work schedule. Fortunately, the manager also wanted two days off. The policy was adjusted to alternating long and short workweeks (six days one week, five days the next). As a result, more candidates are now accepting job offers.
Having worked in HR for a decade, Annie observed that today’s young workers are placing more emphasis on work-life balance: “Even if I have to be a workhorse, I still want to be a happy workhorse.”
A 2023 spring job fair held at the Weishui Campus of Chang'an University (in Xi’an), where some companies explicitly stated their working hours. Jeff’s note: the note describes a five-day work week, with working hours as 8:30AM-11:45AM in the morning and 1:30PM-5:30PM in the afternoon. Source: Perpetual Light Studio.
Annie’s observations may reflect a more general trend.
The article cites data from a Zhaopin survey “New Standards for ‘Good Jobs’: 2025 Workplace Values Survey Report” which finds: “
64.1% of professionals rank ‘work-life balance’ as their top consideration when choosing a job, pushing ‘competitive salary and benefits,’ which long held the top spot, into second place
.” This trend is especially pronounced among respondents born after 1995 or 2000.
From a report by Banyuetan, a magazine published by Xinhua News Agency: “
In the past, graduates primarily sought high salaries and rapid career advancement, often willing to endure intense work pressure for the sake of professional growth. Today’s new generation of young people, however, faces relatively less pressure to survive and places a greater emphasis on personal well-being and quality of life
.”
A few other interesting nuggets from this longform piece:
These preferences may reflect regional differences. Chenyu Wang, an HR staffer for an advertising agency, points out that her company’s Chengdu branch had to switch to a two-day weekend. From the article: “
Wang noted that whenever candidates learned about the six-day workweek during initial online communications, they would immediately vanish, stating: ‘I need my own nightlife; I want to go out and have fun after getting off work.’”
Apparently, Chengdu, China’s westernmost major city, is known for its slower pace of life.
On social media, a faction of “Two-Day Weekend Defenders” [双休侠] has emerged. They urge fellow workers to commit to a two-day weekend as a non-negotiable. Some of these defenders are currently employed but maintain active resumes on job platforms so they can send a message to companies by rejecting positions that only offer one day off.
From the article: “‘
We apologize, but we are currently seeking a position that offers a two-day weekend; your company does not meet our criteria.’ This is the script 33-year-old ‘two-day weekend defender’ Minghuan Wei uses, mimicking the formal rejection language companies often send to job applicants. He has used it to decline six companies that failed to offer a two-day weekend. ‘Fighting the dark arts with the dark arts,’ he says
.”
