The biggest story in Taiwan’s tech industry this week—aside from Jensen Huang’s（黃仁勳）high-profile arrival in Taipei—was undoubtedly the employee bonus backlash at TSMC.
The seriousness of the issue became evident when TSMC Chairman and CEO C.C. Wei（魏哲家） reportedly canceled an overseas trip and urgently organized internal meetings to communicate directly with employees—an unusually rare move for him.
A few days ago, a colleague who manages this newsletter’s social media accounts posted our previous issue—focused on TSMC’s Technology Symposium—on a TSMC employee discussion forum. One of the replies read: “Forget that for now. Employees are furious.”
A senior TSMC employee told me the controversy began when many employees checked their Q1 bonuses and discovered the payout was actually lower than the previous quarter.
“That simply doesn’t make sense,” he said. “TSMC earned more money in Q1 this year.”
Historically, the first quarter is TSMC’s seasonal slow period. But driven by explosive AI demand, the company’s earnings per share still grew 13% sequentially this year—an unusual result for Q1.
Combined with rumors that bonuses had been cut by 15%, as well as headlines about large-scale labor unrest at Samsung Electronics, frustration quickly spiraled across employee forums. Some employees even threatened to stop working overtime or go on strike.
Based on my interviews with TSMC employees and analysis of online discussions, I believe the root cause of the anger lies in the widening compensation gap between senior executives—vice presidents and above—and ordinary employees.
That disparity has created a strong sense of relative deprivation, compounded by fears that TSMC’s massive U.S. expansion plans and future capital expenditures could eventually “eat away” at profitability.
According to local media reports, C.C. Wei promised employees that total annual bonuses this year would increase by 30% from last year.
But TSMC’s earnings per share could very well rise by more than 50% this year. If employee bonuses become structurally “decoupled” from profit growth going forward, it remains unclear whether morale will eventually suffer.
One longtime TSMC veteran from the company’s legendary “mountain-building” era told me employees should think about it differently:
“The money the company earns needs to be reinvested into capacity expansion and technology development. That’s what supports the future stock price. What matters most is whether your total compensation and asset value keep rising.”
He pointed out that TSMC’s stock traded below NT$100 for roughly 12 years because so much profit was continuously reinvested into future expansion.
“People used to criticize TSMC for having a low dividend yield,” he said. “But that’s also why the company was able to scale into what it is today.”
The question now is whether younger generations of TSMC employees—primarily Millennials and Gen Z workers—still share that same willingness to sacrifice short-term gains for the company’s long-term future.
