# ChinAI #360: Anthropic’s Dogma on US-China AI Competition

Dear Professor Ding,
I read your recent ChinAI essay on Anthropic’s framing of U.S.–China AI competition with great interest and, in many respects, considerable agreement.
Your critique of dogmatism in current AI discourse struck me as both perceptive and necessary. I was especially sympathetic to your concern that speculative timelines, asserted with excessive confidence, can harden into a kind of secular eschatology — complete with urgency narratives, moral binaries, and appeals to privileged technical insight. Your analogy to religious forms of thought was, I think, more than merely rhetorical. It identified something psychologically and culturally real in portions of the present AI conversation.
I also found myself agreeing with much of your skepticism regarding simplistic “democratic AI versus authoritarian AI” framings, as well as your emphasis on the historically protracted diffusion of transformative technologies through institutions, economies, and military systems. Your observations regarding electrification, organizational adaptation, logistics, and the slower realities of deployment seem to me important correctives to the more breathless forms of AI futurism.
And yet, after reflecting on your essay, I realized that my own perspective diverges from yours at what may be a deeper conceptual layer.
My sense is that your framework remains primarily institutional and historical: how societies absorb technologies, how states deploy them, and how organizational structures mediate their effects over time. Those questions are obviously indispensable.
But I increasingly suspect that AI may differ from prior general-purpose technologies in one critical respect: it acts recursively upon cognition itself.
Electricity transformed industry. Railroads transformed transportation. The Internet transformed communication. But systems capable of participating in reasoning, synthesis, design, persuasion, and eventually perhaps autonomous discovery may alter the structure of intelligence and human self-understanding in ways that historical analogies only partially capture.
To put it differently, I suspect the deepest implications of AI may ultimately prove less geopolitical than ontological.
That does not mean I accept Anthropic’s timelines, nor do I share the confidence with which some frontier labs forecast imminent “transformative AI.” In fact, I think your critique of that confidence is often justified. But I wonder whether one can simultaneously reject deterministic timelines while still entertaining the possibility that human–machine co-evolution may eventually produce civilizational changes of a genuinely discontinuous kind.
My own interests increasingly revolve around questions of emergence, hybrid cognition, and the evolution of spirituality under conditions of expanding intelligence architectures. I realize that may sound distant from conventional policy discourse, but I suspect these questions will become progressively harder to avoid if AI systems continue advancing beyond narrow instrumental roles.
In any case, I wanted simply to thank you for a thoughtful and intellectually serious essay. Genuine skepticism is valuable, especially at moments when technological discourse begins drifting toward orthodoxy. Even where I diverge from your conclusions, I found your piece clarifying and stimulating.
With best wishes,
David Ellis
