Context:
Back in my senior year of undergrad (2015-2016), when I was studying and interning in Beijing, I picked up a side gig to teach English grammar at an after-school and weekend tutoring program. One of the program’s teachers, who also had a daughter in the class, invited me to lunch before the cycle’s final class, where I politely consumed some Baijiu (Chinese liquor). I still recall, at the last class, one of the kids asking me why my face was so red, as I have a pretty severe case of the Asian flush.
On a Sunday afternoon, these cheeky seven and eight-year-old kids could have been chasing soccer balls out in the sun, and, yet, here they were learning about past participles from someone without any teaching experience in English grammar. Driven by parents who hold tightly onto their children’s education as a meritocratic ladder — as symbolized by the gaokao (college entrance exam) — the private tutoring industry was in the midst of explosive growth, producing unicorn companies like VIPKID and Yuanfudao.
Then, in July 2021, the Chinese government banned for-profit private tutoring. Concerned about the pressures on overworked students and the escalating costs of education (with the assumption that limiting tutoring costs would incentivize parents to have more babies), the government also prohibited online platforms that allowed “companies to hire
tutors from outside of China who are native English speakers
.” The threat to the tutoring industry was existential,
reported
Yi-Ling Liu for
The New Yorker
:
New Oriental let go of sixty thousand of its staff, the online English-tutoring platform VIPKid shut down some of its services, and TAL Education Group, renowned for its Math Olympiad-style courses, transitioned to a “quality education” program that teaches calligraphy instead of calculus. One of China’s largest education companies, Yuanfudao, started posting job listings for fashion designers, sparking rumors that it was pivoting its business to winter jackets.
In this week’s feature translation, we learn about how one platform, Zebra English [斑马], and its AI teacher Jessica have adapted. In a longform piece,
Renwu
[人物] gives us the perspective of the parents who sit by their kid’s side (sometimes literally) as they take the English language journey. Backed by Tencent in 2017, Zebra English is a subsidiary of Yuanfudao.
Key Passages: A longtime flaw in China’s English learning environment is the problem of “Mute English [哑巴英语].”
Parents don’t want their kids to learn Mute English like they once did.
Based in Nanchang, Jiangxi Province, Lin Lan is the mother of a second-grade daughter. Lin graduated from a prestigious Chinese university known for foreign language studies. She always passed the English language tests with flying colors (“a shining star in the exam-oriented education system”).
Yet, when it came to speaking English, she froze. She tells
Renwu
: “
Once, walking on the streets of Beijing, two foreigners asked her for directions. She waved her hands frantically, like someone who couldn’t understand English, and hid behind her husband. Her husband’s English was barely at the CET-4 level, but he managed to communicate with them using broken, accented ‘survival English,’ gesturing until the foreigners understood
.”
Another issue is the ruthless competition over English teachers.
For over half a year, Xu Ming set her alarm to wake up at 3AM, in order to open up an app and book English classes for her child. She had to scramble to reserve Anna, one of the top Filipino English teachers
on the platform.
Over time, Anna got assigned more administrative work and taught fewer classes, until she eventually disappeared from Xu Ming’s booking list. Xu Ming felt helpless: “
On the other hand, any uncertain news from the Philippines also weighs heavily on her mind. For example, news reports of typhoons or earthquakes in the Philippines would cause her anxiety, worrying about her teacher’s safety and the disruption to teaching. She constantly worries that this delicate transnational educational cable might suddenly snap due to some distant disturbance
.”
Zhao Li also felt powerless when she tried to find an in-person English teacher for her son. She had finally negotiated with an English teacher working at a local school in southern Hubei, but the teacher did not come to the agreed-upon trial lesson and said that they were going to Shanghai to teach because the pay was higher there.
A generation that desperately wants their children to learn English better than they did meets a new technology that promises a solution to these problems.
In early 2023, the Zebra English team decided to develop an AI-powered tutor for spoken English.
To train AI teacher “Jessica”, they cultivated a warm personality. The research team would conduct feedback sessions, in which they played “
the role of students, deliberately giving incorrect, tricky, or even whimsical answers to observe and correct Jessica’s reactions
.” They also reviewed 25-minute trial lessons with children, analyzing Jessica’s responses for each situation.
Jessica’s design. Source:
Renwu.
According to Zebra English’s Chief Product Officer, Jiaming Xiu, one of the most difficult hurdles in the process was Jessica’s ability to accurately hear kid’s speech in a real-world, noisy home environment. Xiu told
Renwu
, “
Children’s vocal organs are not fully developed; their pitch is higher, their pronunciation is less clear, and their speech is full of unpredictable and illogical babbling. General-purpose speech recognition models often struggle with these non-standard speech sounds
.” To address this, Zebra leveraged their children’s speech database as well as a contextual prediction strategy.
The hope behind AI tutors like Jessica is that they will help break through the Mute English wall.
Zhang Ming’s study was one of the testing grounds for Jessica. Based in Beijing, Zhang had grown frustrated with what he thought was the perfect fit for his son Zhuozhuo’s English language learning: a high-end (500RMB per session) in-person class with a one-to-four teacher-to-student ratio. However, his son would was easily distracted and Zhang noticed that his son had not grasped a certain concept before the lesson moved on. During the tutoring session with Jessica, he sat by Zhuozhuo’s side. He states, “
My son is afraid of people, afraid of making mistakes in front of others, but he’s more relaxed in front of Jessica than when facing a live English teacher.” After shedding his psychological burden, the pressure to speak decreased dramatically
."
Let’s end with the experience of Xu Ming, the 3AM alarm-setter.
Renwu
relays:
The most dramatic scene occurred one Saturday at the soccer practice field. Xu Ming, not particularly interested in her son’s soccer playing, was scrolling through her phone out of boredom when her son, who had just finished drinking water, stood in front of her, his face flushed and his expression serious. After a brief standoff, instead of complaining in Chinese, he looked directly at his mother and said in clear, fluent English, “You should look at me, not at your phone.” This sudden “accusation” didn’t embarrass Xu Ming; instead, it made her feel quite gratified. “At that moment, I knew in my heart that this had worked.” His English expression was beginning to feel natural.
My take: It’s still way too early to tell whether Zebra English’s model will work at scale, but I thought the
Renwu
article convincingly demonstrated why families would seek out this particular AI application. It was weird to me that
Renwu
did not mention the 2021 policy at all, which seems like a big gap in background context for readers. Maybe it’s too sensitive of a topic, but
Renwu
has done some investigative reporting pieces in the past.

The First AI English Teacher “Takes Office”
In the aforementioned
New Yorker
article, Yi-Ling Liu points out the flaws in China’s crackdown on private tutoring:
Skeptics have been watching to see how double reduction will work in practice. Yan Yifei, a social-policy researcher at the London School of Economics, believes that as long as China’s highly competitive college-entrance examination—the
gaokao
—still decides who gets into élite schools, students and parents will seek every advantage. Already the tutoring industry has continued underground, with rich families hiring expensive private tutors as “professional nannies.”
Tracking gender representation across 268 events and 210 podcast episodes organized by 63 European think tanks, the Women Insight on China (WiCH) initiative finds: “men made up roughly two-thirds of all speakers, while women accounted for just 34.7 percent.”
The New York Times
reports on an essential aspect of U.S.-China AI competition:
In 2020, a study from the Paulson Institute, which promotes constructive ties between the United States and China, estimated that Chinese A.I. researchers accounted for nearly one-third of the world’s top A.I. talent. Most of those Chinese researchers worked for American companies and universities.
A new study from the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace shows that a vast majority of these Chinese researchers have continued to work for U.S. institutions. Of the 100 top-tier Chinese researchers in the original study who were at U.S. universities or companies in 2019 — three years before the arrival of ChatGPT set off the global A.I. boom — 87 are still doing research at U.S. universities or companies.

But analysts like Mr. Sheehan argue that the risk of espionage is far outweighed by the benefits of hiring and collaborating with Chinese talent. They worry that if the Trump administration expands its crackdown on Chinese talent in the United States, the move could seriously harm U.S. research.

Amid months of Trump administration wrangling over immigration policy, nearly all of those schools said that concerns about obtaining student visas contributed to falling enrollments, and more than two-thirds pointed to travel restrictions
