The biggest story since June has been U.S. President Donald Trump’s decision, citing national security concerns, to order foreign nationals to temporarily stop using the latest and most powerful AI models from Anthropic, currently the world’s leading AI company (the ban was lifted in early July), sending shockwaves through the entire AI industry.
Many countries and companies suddenly woke up to the enormous risks of becoming overly dependent on American cloud-based AI models. As a result, the concept of “Sovereign AI” — owning both software and hardware capabilities independently — has once again surged in popularity.
“After the Anthropic incident, even people inside Trend Micro（趨勢科技） have started asking themselves: if there were no Anthropic and no OpenAI, would we simply stop writing code?” said Trend Micro CTO Oscar Chang（張偉欽）. As a result, the company has already developed its own AI models for internal use.
His other role is chairman of Magna AI, a startup jointly established by Trend Micro and the Wistron Group（緯創） in October last year and registered in Japan. The company focuses on providing end-to-end Sovereign AI export services and represents a bold experiment by two of Taiwan’s leading AI software and hardware companies.
Its CEO is Dr. Moataz Bin Ali, a Saudi national and former head of Trend Micro’s Middle East R&D operations. The company has already signed six memorandums of understanding (MOUs) in Malaysia and the Middle East.
Among them, Malaysian technology group Zchwantech is planning to invest US$700 million to build a 20MW AI data center through Magna. (Taiwan’s largest AI data center today, Foxconn’s supercomputing center in Kaohsiung’s Asia New Bay Area, has a capacity of 27MW.)
“We’re still getting inquiries from many companies,” Chang said.
The idea for Magna originated from Trend Micro CEO Eva Chen（陳怡樺）, who wanted to help developing countries use AI to solve problems that traditional approaches could not, such as building AI assistants for operating rooms in Middle Eastern hospitals.
In other words, the company originally intended to focus only on the outermost layer of Jensen Huang’s “five-layer AI cake theory” — applications.
“At the beginning, we didn’t want to do all five layers of the cake,” Chang said. But customers would respond by saying, “I don’t even have GPUs or servers. How am I supposed to do AI?”
As a result, Magna had no choice but to help customers with the other “four layers of the cake” as well: models, infrastructure, chips, and energy. The company quickly adjusted its business model,
moving away from the somewhat vague concept of a “global AI transformation factory for the entire value chain” and toward the simpler and more memorable slogan: “Build your own AI.”
Our concept is essentially an AI deployment team,” he said, similar to what Wistron is doing by bringing together Taiwan’s leading AI supply-chain partners and taking them overseas as an integrated solution provider.
But being able to do this has relied heavily on Taiwan’s dramatically improved international image in recent years.
“Today, people are willing to recognize Taiwan as an AI powerhouse,” Chang said. “A few years ago, that might not have been the case.”
