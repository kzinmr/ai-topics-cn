Hello everyone,
The biggest news this week is, of course, that MediaTek—Taiwan’s second-largest tech company by market capitalization—hit the daily limit up for three consecutive days. The rally grew so intense that regulators designated the stock for special handling (tightening trading restrictions), triggering a sharp pullback at one point.
Even so, MediaTek’s market cap now stands at NT$5.82 trillion, just 15% behind its long-dominant rival Qualcomm. At its current momentum, a golden cross could occur as soon as next week.
“It feels like a dream,” one MediaTek engineer remarked.
A former semiconductor industry executive believes MediaTek is undergoing a re-rating—similar to what Delta Electronics experienced last year.
The market has begun to recognize MediaTek’s gains in custom silicon design for Google’s TPU, repositioning it as a major AI semiconductor player comparable to Broadcom, rather than an Asian smartphone IC vendor locked in a stagnating handset market battle with Qualcomm. This shift appears to be prompting several international funds to increase their holdings, fueling the surge.
“Rick (CEO Rick Tsai)’s efforts are finally being recognized,” the executive said.
Having tracked Qualcomm and MediaTek for over a decade, this resonates deeply.
When MediaTek first entered the smartphone market, Qualcomm barely took it seriously.
Even recently, a senior Taiwan-born Qualcomm executive told me that MediaTek was simply not comparable—too far behind in both talent depth and technological foundation.
A few years ago, industry veterans often made similar claims about TSMC versus Intel.
Yet as long as you pick the right battlefield—and pair it with first-rate execution—so-called “B-tier” teams can catch up, and even surpass U.S. “A-tier” players.
That is the key lesson I’ve taken from closely observing TSMC and MediaTek over the past few years.

Friday also coincided with Phison’s earnings call.
One recurring concern around the company, especially amid this capacity-constrained memory boom, is that Phison is the only one among Taiwan’s “Big Four” memory players without its own fabs.
Its current high margins are largely driven by “buy low, sell high.” Once last year’s low-cost inventory is depleted and replaced with higher-cost supply, critics argue, its true profitability will be exposed.
But in reality, Phison has spent the past several years investing roughly 20% of its annual revenue into R&D—building an entirely new product line.
Not only is it riding the AI wave, it is also transitioning toward becoming a system-level provider, breaking away from the traditional memory module business model.
