But I want to start with this: U.S. action camera leader GoPro has announced a 23% global workforce reduction following continued losses.
Another relic of a fading era.
The company has close ties with Taiwan’s supply chain, with manufacturing primarily handled by Chicony Electronics（群光電子）, and Foxconn having previously taken a stake.
The reasons for its decline are, in fact, similar to what I wrote in a previous newsletter about robotic vacuum pioneer iRobot—it failed to keep up with the “iteration speed” of Chinese competitors DJI（大疆） and Insta360.
First, DJI’s drones have carved out part of the outdoor photography market.
Meanwhile, Insta360’s panoramic cameras, powered by superior software capabilities—such as “shoot first, frame later” and the “invisible selfie stick”—have left GoPro struggling to catch up.
This brings to mind a well-known Chinese tech podcast I once listened to. Several next-generation entrepreneurs asserted on the show that “consumer electronics (including EVs) will inevitably be dominated by China.”
Because, backed by China’s speed and execution, “once we iterate, they (U.S., Japanese, and Korean competitors) simply can’t keep up.”
Another recent development seems to validate this claim.
Sony Honda Mobility, a joint venture between Sony and Honda, announced at the end of March that it would halt development of its electric vehicle “Afeela 1,” which had originally been slated for launch this year.
Sony had envisioned Afeela as an extension of its PlayStation ecosystem, turning the car cabin into “a space for enjoying animation and games.” When it was first announced four years ago, I was optimistic that this “dream team” could help Japan regain ground in EVs.
The reason for failure: it was simply too slow. Four years on, the car still has not been released.
In contrast, Chinese competitor Xiaomi, a newcomer to automotive manufacturing, announced its EV ambitions in 2021 and began deliveries on schedule within three years. It has now sold hundreds of thousands of units.
