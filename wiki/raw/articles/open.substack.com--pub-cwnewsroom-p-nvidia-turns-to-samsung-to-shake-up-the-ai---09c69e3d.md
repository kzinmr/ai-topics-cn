# Nvidia Turns to Samsung to Shake Up the AI Substrate Market. Unimicron and Kinsus Face a New Threat

Source: https://open.substack.com/pub/cwnewsroom/p/nvidia-turns-to-samsung-to-shake-up-the-ai-substrate-market?utm_source=substack&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=email-restack-comment&action=restack-comment&r=2flx6&token=eyJ1c2VyX2lkIjo0MDg3NDgyLCJwb3N0X2lkIjoyMjE3NTMzOTcsImlhdCI6MTc5Mjc0ODE4NywiZXhwIjoxNzk1MzQwMTg3LCJpc3MiOiJwdWItMzQyMzQwOCIsInN1YiI6InBvc3QtcmVhY3Rpb24ifQ.WUX4eATE-IYEm701f5RF0gatp0H5ZWECkGSMOPkO9NI&utm_source=substack&utm_medium=email

Hello everyone,
Allow me to indulge in a little self-promotion. In last week’s newsletter,
I exclusively revealed an unusual phenomenon: Nvidia was providing substantial financial backing to Unimicron（欣興）, IBIDEN, King Yuan Electronics（京元電）, and Anker to support their capacity expansions.
Less than a week later,
Nvidia issued a press release at its GTC conference in Berlin that effectively confirmed my reporting
, announcing that it was partnering with these suppliers to accelerate the buildout of AI infrastructure.
At the time, I asked in my newsletter, “What is Jensen Huang（黃仁勳） so nervous about?”
The answer was right there in the press release.
“We are in the midst of the largest infrastructure buildout in history. Demand is extraordinary, but capacity is the constraint,” Huang said.
IC substrates, in particular.
More precisely, ABF substrates used in TSMC’s CoWoS packaging—the third layer, the “S” (substrate), in CoWoS’s sandwich-like structure.
That is why Nvidia’s press release opened by announcing agreements with three substrate manufacturers
to expand their combined capacity to 2.5 times its current level over the next three years.
This marked the first time the world’s most valuable company had publicly committed to securing substrate capacity, a development of considerable significance.
In fact, just two days earlier, Kinsus（景碩） CEO and President Ho-hsu Chen（陳河旭）, whose company supplies substrates to Nvidia, made a striking prediction in an exclusive interview with me.
“The final bottleneck in the entire AI supply chain will definitely be substrates,” he said. “Substrates will inevitably be in short supply at the end.”
Why is he so certain?
Read on in this week’s newsletter.
First, a brief introduction to Kinsus, Taiwan’s third-largest substrate manufacturer—and the one widely seen as having the strongest growth prospects.
That optimism stems from a corporate shake-up earlier this year, whose repercussions are still reverberating through the industry.
At the beginning of the year, T.J. Tseng（曾子章）, the former chairman who had personally led Unimicron to become the world’s second-largest substrate manufacturer, was abruptly replaced by parent company UMC. Former UMC Co-President Shan-chieh Chien（簡山傑） took over as chairman.
The move triggered a chain reaction. Industry sources say TSMC, which had previously worked closely with Unimicron, subsequently turned to Kinsus to collaborate on the development of new packaging technologies and materials.
As a result, Kinsus, long regarded as a second-tier substrate manufacturer, gained an opportunity to enter Nvidia’s supply chain. It is now seen as a potential third supplier after IBIDEN and Unimicron.
“Nvidia is now grooming Kinsus as a supplier,” said an industry peer. Kinsus shares have consequently risen 5.5-fold since the beginning of the year, making it the best-performing stock among Taiwan’s three leading substrate manufacturers.
The pace of Kinsus’s capacity expansion is so aggressive that it is approaching that of industry leader Unimicron.
As reported in the previous newsletter, Unimicron spent NT$10.08 billion in early October to acquire Bridgestone’s former factory in Hukou, Hsinchu, setting a record for factory sales in northern Taiwan’s industrial parks in recent years.
Around the same time, Kinsus spent NT$6 billion to acquire the former Chu Shui Che（掬水軒） factory in Taoyuan’s Pingzhen Industrial Park.
Industry sources also say Kinsus was the company that competed fiercely with Unimicron for the former Bridgestone facility, ultimately losing the bid despite submitting the second-highest offer.
During his keynote speech at the Taiwan Printed Circuit Association Show (TPCA Show 2026), Unimicron Chairman Shan-chieh Chien identified a structural problem: insufficient capacity expansion.
Using 2024 as the base year, he noted that annual capital expenditures by major cloud service providers (CSPs) had increased to 6 to 6.8 times their 2024 levels by this year, while substrate capacity had only doubled.
“Because everyone got burned by Apple in the past,” Ho-hsu Chen explained.
As a result, manufacturers remain deeply wary of the ambitious demand forecasts presented by customers.
“Almost everyone got burned in the last cycle. Now they’re afraid that after building a factory, the customer’s demand won’t last long enough to support it.”
But with Jensen Huang deploying both orders and financial support, Taiwan’s substrate manufacturers have now shifted into a race to catch up.
“I need to build a factory every three quarters,” Chen said.
Kinsus began catching up with TSMC’s 5nm process, which is used to manufacture Nvidia’s H100.
“I have five generations to catch up on: 5, 3, 2, 1.4, and 1,” he said. “I already have 5nm, and next year I need to move to 3nm.”
That raised a question for me. Since when did substrate manufacturers have to keep pace with Moore’s Law?
Chen explained that this was actually a simplified way of describing the situation.
In recent years, each generation of TSMC’s advanced process technology has been accompanied by corresponding advances in packaging technology.
As package sizes increase and I/O interconnects become denser, substrate manufacturers must simultaneously make substantial improvements in factory cleanliness, equipment precision, and the number of substrate layers.
That means installing entirely new equipment and building new factories capable of meeting the requirements of each generation.
Moreover,
virtually every new TSMC factory creates demand for a corresponding new substrate factory.
During its third-quarter earnings call, TSMC said it would build 13 fabs in Taiwan and another five to six overseas over the next several years.
According to Ho-hsu Chen, this effectively means that 18 new ABF substrate factories for AI applications will need to be built before 2030.
But “there is no land left in northern Taiwan, and the chances of completing the capacity expansion before 2030 are slim.” Kinsus is even considering expanding into southern Taiwan.
That is why Chen insists that “the final bottleneck will definitely be substrate manufacturers.”
This comes despite recent warnings of shortages in upstream substrate materials and equipment, with lead times for some equipment reaching as long as 31 months.
Chen nevertheless believes that shortages of upstream materials and equipment will ease relatively quickly, potentially within two to three years.
“Land is the real constraint,” he said.
As a result,
ABF substrates used in AI chips could face severe shortages before 2031.
This was Chen’s first prediction during our interview.
His second prediction is that once these 18 factories are completed, the current wave of massive capacity expansion among substrate manufacturers—and even the AI data centers behind that expansion—could come to an end.
According to Chen, a technology roadmap recently provided to Kinsus by a major U.S. customer suggests that around 2030 or 2031, large AI chips used in the cloud will become a “niche market.”
AI inference will primarily be handled by smaller edge chips, meaning demand will shift toward consumer devices.
These consumer-oriented chips can be manufactured using existing factories, eliminating the need to build new ones.
Chen compared data center demand to the earlier rollout of mobile base stations.
“When 4G and 5G first arrived, everyone was building base stations like crazy. But you don’t keep building them indefinitely. Once you reach a certain point, the pace gradually slows.”
His third prediction: Taiwanese manufacturers will displace their Japanese rivals and come to dominate the high-end substrate market.
Chen argued that geopolitical considerations would prevent major U.S. AI companies from using substrates manufactured in mainland China. Meanwhile, Japan’s IBIDEN has been expanding capacity too slowly.
Once Taiwanese manufacturers dramatically increase their capacity over the next two to three years, the high-end substrate market “will belong to Taiwanese companies,” he said.
He expects Taiwanese manufacturers to capture more than 50% of the market within two years at the latest.
But after the interview, events took a dramatic turn.
Chen may have made that prediction too soon.
Share
First,
when industry insiders say that “major AI companies won’t use substrates from mainland China,”
they are referring to Zhen Ding Technology（臻鼎）, a Foxconn subsidiary and the world’s largest PCB group, whose substrate production capacity is concentrated almost entirely in mainland China.
Yet Zhen Ding Chairman Charles Shen（沈慶芳） made a startling statement at a press conference during the TPCA Show.
He said that shortages had become so severe that U.S. customers were beginning to change their attitude toward Zhen Ding.
“In the past, they insisted on OOC (Out of China). Now, if there’s no fish, shrimp will do. As long as there’s supply.”
Although Shen was referring specifically to the printed circuit board business, a senior Zhen Ding executive told me that U.S. customers were also becoming more flexible about substrates.
Evidence of this shift can be found in the prospectus filed in Hong Kong this July by Leading Interconnect Semiconductor Technology（禮鼎半導體科技）, the company into which Zhen Ding spun off its Chinese IC substrate business.
Leading generated just RMB 900 million in revenue in the first quarter of this year, less than half that of Kinsus.
Yet the share of its business attributable to “AI and HPC” surged from 2.3% in 2023 to 32.6% in the first quarter of this year.
How much of that business comes from U.S. AI data centers remains to be clarified.
If Chinese production capacity is allowed into the supply chain, however, the competitive landscape for high-end substrates will inevitably undergo a major shake-up.
But the development that shocked Kinsus—and indeed Taiwan’s entire substrate industry—was the unexpected appearance of a new contender in Nvidia’s latest press release.
Of the three substrate manufacturers that signed agreements with Nvidia, two were IBIDEN and Unimicron. But the third was not Kinsus, as the Taiwanese industry had expected. Instead, it was SEMCO—Samsung Electro-Mechanics, a Samsung Group company and the world’s third-largest substrate manufacturer.
The press release explicitly stated that “Samsung Electro-Mechanics is joining Nvidia’s substrate supply chain,” immediately bringing existing capacity at its Sejong and Busan facilities in South Korea into Nvidia’s supply network.
By 2028, Nvidia is also expected to account for a “significant” share of Samsung Electro-Mechanics’ substrate business.
“Very unexpected,” a Kinsus executive told me in shock. “Korean manufacturers have never been part of our discussions.”
The executive said the company needed time to digest the news.
Previously, a senior executive at a major manufacturer had assured me that Samsung Electro-Mechanics would be unable to enter Nvidia’s substrate supply chain. One of the main reasons, he said, was its inability to secure materials.
Upstream substrate materials are largely dominated by Japanese suppliers. Because of longstanding national rivalries between Japan and South Korea, Japanese companies would quietly put Korean manufacturers at a disadvantage.
An executive at Nittobo even told Taiwanese manufacturers that the company prioritized supplying Taiwan over South Korea.
As a result, when the senior executive heard that Samsung Electro-Mechanics had signed an agreement with Nvidia, he initially insisted it was “fake news.”
According to South Korean media reports, Nvidia plans to invest as much as KRW 2.6 trillion to secure advanced substrate capacity for the next five years. Tesla is also investing KRW 1.1 trillion, bringing the total investment to KRW 3.7 trillion.
The investment will establish dedicated AI substrate production lines at Samsung Electro-Mechanics’ Sejong and Busan facilities, with deliveries beginning in 2026.
KRW 3.7 trillion is equivalent to approximately NT$91 billion. Although this is slightly less than the JPY 500 billion that Nvidia and Intel prepaid to support IBIDEN’s capacity expansion, the remarkable development could reshape the AI substrate supply chain into a market divided among Taiwan, Japan, and South Korea.
What is more, the “catfish” Nvidia has introduced into the market belongs to the Samsung Electronics group, which has recently been making enormous profits from memory chips.
Given Samsung’s historically aggressive approach to competition, Taiwan’s three leading substrate manufacturers could face some tough competition ahead.
This reminded me of a video that went viral online a few months ago, showing Jensen Huang eating fried chicken at a small restaurant in Seoul with Samsung Electronics Chairman Jay Y. Lee and others.
At the time, I assumed Huang was simply trying to secure memory supplies.
But a veteran electronics industry executive told me that Huang had deliberately staged the scene for TSMC, sending a message to C.C. Wei（魏哲家） that Nvidia was not entirely dependent on Taiwan.
Could Nvidia’s partnership with Samsung Electro-Mechanics therefore be just the opening episode of a major drama set to unfold in 2027—one in which Jensen Huang moves closer to South Korea while Samsung launches a counteroffensive against Taiwanese manufacturers?
We’ll find out soon enough.
