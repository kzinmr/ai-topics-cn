Hello everyone,
The recent decision by Taiwan’s KMT and TPP opposition parties to cut the national defense budget for military drone procurement has drawn widespread public attention.
In response, the Executive Yuan recently approved a draft special act that proposes a special budget of NT$210 billion to restore funding for the procurement of 210,000 drones and suicide unmanned surface vessels. The proposal effectively restores the previously cut procurement funding and has sent shares of related companies, including Thunder Tiger（雷虎）, soaring to their daily limit.
The ruling party’s public position is that the budget will use domestic demand to nurture an export-oriented industry, enabling Taiwanese manufacturers not only to strengthen Taiwan’s defense capabilities but also to leverage their “non-China” advantage to enter the supply chains of democratic countries around the world.
But what are the export prospects for Taiwan’s drone industry?
In my view, comments made by Cat Buchatskiy, Director of Analysis at the prominent Ukrainian military think tank Snake Island, during a well-known episode of the ChinaTalk podcast with the host and
Chip War
author Chris Miller, are worth considering.
First, China is currently supporting both Ukraine's and Russia's drone industries.
TAF, previously Ukraine’s largest drone manufacturer, found it puzzling when sourcing cameras, batteries, and other components in Shenzhen. Chinese suppliers would sometimes leave its representatives waiting in meeting rooms for extended periods or hurriedly ask them to leave via the stairs.
The reason was simple: Russian drone companies were visiting the same factories at the same time, and Chinese suppliers did not want Ukrainian and Russian delegations to run into each other and create an awkward situation.
Second, that does not mean China will continue providing such support indefinitely. Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi once remarked, “If we had really intervened, this war would have ended long ago.”
Third, as a result, Ukraine has remained vigilant and is actively reducing its dependence on China. Today, 99% of drones are assembled domestically in Ukraine, eliminating the need to import complete systems.
Although components still rely heavily on Chinese suppliers, Ukraine has gradually localized production and can now assemble motors and batteries domestically. It even plans to mine the rare earth materials required for motor magnets on its own.
Ukraine’s domestic drone industry is now capable of producing as many as 400,000 drones per month.
Once the war eventually comes to an end—or even begins to ease—
it is reasonable to expect that Ukrainian-made drones, proven under the harshest battlefield conditions, will flood military markets across the democratic world.
Taiwanese manufacturers should therefore avoid being overly optimistic.

The current AI boom has created a series of unexpected phenomena.
“So many things that we never imagined would be in short supply have suddenly become scarce,” one industry executive told me.
A highly cyclical industry like memory entering a “supercycle” is understandable. But even IC substrates, printed circuit boards, copper-clad laminates, and passive components have all been swept into severe shortages.
That has helped companies such as Unimicron（欣興） and Elite Material (EMC)（台光電） reach trillion-dollar market capitalizations in New Taiwan dollars, while YAGEO（國巨） Chairman Pierre Chen（陳泰銘） has become Taiwan’s richest person.
So who will be the next “unexpected shortage”?
Some believe it could be silicon carbide—the semiconductor industry’s dream material that, at the moment, is causing losses for nearly every company involved.
