Hello everyone,
The biggest story this week was, of course, the Trump–Xi summit.
Perhaps the most surreal image came from Jensen Huang: wearing a backpack and waiting on the tarmac at an Alaska airport for Air Force One to refuel before boarding, becoming the final member to join the U.S. business delegation.
His dramatic last-minute addition to the trip immediately reignited speculation over whether the United States may further relax export restrictions on Nvidia GPUs.
Before that discussion, however, one question needs to be clarified:
Has the U.S. embargo on Nvidia’s most advanced GPUs effectively constrained China’s AI development?
Judging from the recently unveiled DeepSeek V4—released after more than a year of delay—the answer appears to be yes.
According to multiple benchmark evaluations, the model trails the most advanced U.S. models by as much as eight months in performance, widening the gap versus R1, which shocked the world during last year’s Lunar New Year period.
Is this the result of being forced to train models using domestic Huawei Ascend chips?
In mid-April, an article published by “Yuyuan Tantian,”（玉淵譚天） a commentary platform affiliated with China Central Television (CCTV), drew significant attention.
One sentence stood out: “After adapting to Ascend chips, the DeepSeek-V4 model achieved high-throughput, low-latency inference deployment.”
Notice the wording carefully: it only referred to “inference.” What about model training? The article said nothing.
Yet in February, Trump administration officials told Reuters that DeepSeek’s V4 had been trained using Nvidia’s most advanced Blackwell GPUs at a computing center in Inner Mongolia.
That, of course, would have relied on smuggling. And because it operates through underground channels, the scale is naturally limited—nothing comparable to the tens of thousands of GPUs deployed by major U.S. cloud providers.
Still, the fact that DeepSeek chose this route suggests that even
China’s leading AI companies remain unable to break free from dependence on Nvidia’s CUDA ecosystem when it comes to model training.
Back to this week’s newsletter.
Recently, a major CoWoS supplier told me that since the rise of Agentic AI, major players across the industry have sharply raised shipment forecasts.
The problem is that supply of critical upstream materials has not increased.
The next bottleneck may not be advanced chips or HBM, but the substrates needed to package them.
In the coming AI supply crunch, companies that can secure substrate supply will hold the advantage.
And this is precisely where MediaTek made a surprisingly aggressive move ahead of both Broadcom and Nvidia.
That move could help MediaTek gain further favor with Google—and potentially secure even more AI ASIC orders in the future.
