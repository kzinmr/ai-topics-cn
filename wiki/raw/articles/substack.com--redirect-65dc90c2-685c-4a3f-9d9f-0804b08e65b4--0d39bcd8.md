# China Is Winning the AI Race Trump Won’t Run

Source: https://substack.com/redirect/65dc90c2-685c-4a3f-9d9f-0804b08e65b4?j=eyJ1IjoiMmZseDYifQ.6O_iC1_GWCzChnbrHzSrs1hMpJpoODTUJm_FAk3tf6E

It matters who writes the rules of the road as artificial intelligence is deployed in every industry in our lives, but the rulebook of policies, regulations and ethical guidelines could look very different depending on who writes it. China and the United States should be fighting for the pen, but America is barely in the running.
China wants to work with the U.S. to jointly govern
AI
, according to its leader,
Xi Jinping
, who said in his recent state visit to Washington, D.C., that both nations had a responsibility to “manage AI for good.” “Good” for Beijing means AI models not only must remain under human control but also under the oversight of the
Chinese Communist Party
(CCP).
This summer, China created the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization, or WAICO. This global AI governing body was designed to capture small and middle-income powers that find themselves outside the U.S. tech boom for political and ideological reasons and for their lack of relevant industry and resources. Beijing is offering
these countries a say
in AI rule-making because it knows the technology will only be as powerful as the framework that controls it.
In America, the world’s leading AI labs are shouting the loudest for regulation. President
Donald Trump
sees an irony in that and believes whoever makes the best AI will get to make the rules too. He’s half right: the global AI rule-maker will need buy-in from its users too—and China is already halfway there.
The Other AI Race
Technology has always outpaced regulation; it was the case with the internet and it will be the same with AI. The world may not be asking enough questions about who should govern AI and how, but the potential answers are already beginning to fragment along geopolitical lines.
Since 2018, China has hosted the World Artificial Intelligence Conference in Shanghai with the aim of institutionalizing its role in governing the technology now and in the future. This past July, Xi oversaw the creation of the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization, or WAICO, a 37-member group of low- and middle-income countries from the Global South.
Many a WAICO member finds itself on America’s AI cooperation blacklist: Cuba, Iran and Russia are unlikely to meet U.S. trust standards for technology sharing, ever. But if the United Nations has taught China anything, it’s the utility of securing votes from the forgotten corners of the world to legitimize its positions and demonstrate the hypocrisy of U.S. values.
U.N. Secretary-General António Guterres attended WAICO’s inauguration and praised China for including the Global South. Technology “cannot be governed by a handful of countries or a handful of companies,” he said.
Observers say China moved to formalize WAICO after the U.S. established Pax Silica in December, 2025. The coalition of West-aligned countries seeks secure, end-to-end supply chains for the
full AI tech stack
, from data centers and power grids to critical minerals and advanced computer chips.
Aside from mineral-rich Kazakhstan, whose resources make it valuable to both rival AI blocs, Pax Silica’s 24 signatories are mostly high-income economies whose accelerated AI industries are threatening to leave the rest behind. The group’s economic security mandate is to reinforce the U.S.’ systemic advantages over
China
, but it lacks a voice on AI governance or safety.
The U.S. appears dismissive of WAICO, which for now is without concrete commitments beyond the principle of governance. But that won’t worry Beijing as long as Washington offers no viable alternative. It means the China-led body could become the first multinational institution to set agendas and draft treaties in the name of good AI without America’s input.
Beijing is offering its friends something arguably more valuable than a seat at the table: the technology itself. Outside of China, WAICO’s members have little to no AI industry and therefore are the perfect candidates to adopt the Chinese AI stack. Beijing is only too happy to spread its tech through existing channels like the
Belt and Road Initiative
infrastructure export program.
“If Chinese firms become major providers of AI models and their corresponding cloud services, data centers, and energy grids, then China may also gain influence over technical standards, data practices, content controls, and what defines responsible AI in the first place,” emerging technologies consultant Hugh Harsono
wrote
in The Diplomat on Tuesday.
China could dilute U.S. leadership in AI through pure scale by ensuring Chinese labs have more customers than their American competitors, and by encouraging friendly governments to adopt its rules.
China would love for America to join WAICO and bolster its credentials as AI governor, but it benefits from the opposite too: Beijing is poised to tell the world that it tried and failed to convince the U.S. to help make AI safer together.
Why China Needs AI Control
Beijing has spent years preparing for this moment. In the last six months, the Chinese government has issued guidelines for the development of AI agents, made rules on the behavior of human-like AI services and announced plans to regulate data used in embodied AI—software carried inside physical bodies like
humanoid robots or drones
.
China was always going to be a first mover on regulation because it has
deep concerns
about AI’s ability to empower the public to create and share unsanctioned information online, directly or indirectly fueling narratives that could undermine the CCP’s authority.
Like in Iran, Chinese embassies and state media outlets have used AI in their social media-driven propaganda campaigns against the West, but they’re much more conservative back home. Xi doesn’t have a social media account, but even if he did, he wouldn’t be posting the type of generative AI content that Trump has popularized on Truth Social.
The U.S. president has fully embraced the technology for its creative and economic potential and is convinced the AI industry will benefit from a small government approach. He warned Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and others that the AI industry would collapse under the
weight of regulation
—and that China would win.
In China, where mass communication and strict online censorship by the state have coexisted for three decades, its leaders don’t have the luxury of waiting around to see
what AI could do
next.
Chen Yixin, who as minister of state security is China’s top spy, said in an essay last month that
the CCP’s answer
to Western concerns about rogue AI agents was to ensure total Party authority over the technology in order to safeguard the core values of state power, information control and social stability, in the pursuit of regime security.
Google DeepMind cofounder Demis Hassabis wants the U.S. to lead a global AI standards group, Sam Altman of OpenAI wants the technology to be governed by a members-led organization like the International Atomic Energy Agency, and Anthropic’s Amodei wants AI models to be scrutinized by a technical body like the Federal Aviation Administration.
China now has the answer to all three, and the rules it makes at home are ready for export.
“The United States cannot credibly propose international standards for regulating frontier AI while at the same time lacking a coherent national set of regulations,” tech analysts Chris Collins and Aalok Mehta
said
in a report last month for the Center for Strategic and International Studies.
“China has realized this and is therefore making a dedicated AI law. Therefore, developing a federal framework to regulate AI would give U.S. diplomacy a model to offer other countries,” they said.
